\abstract{
Machine-learning models based on self-reported health indicators can support diabetes risk screening, but their evaluation requires more than reporting classification accuracy because class imbalance, decision-threshold choice, probability calibration, and model interpretation can lead to different conclusions about model performance. This study analyzed 253,680 records from the unweighted CDC BRFSS 2015 Diabetes Health Indicators dataset, in which reported prediabetes or diabetes accounted for 13.93\% of the sample. Logistic Regression, Decision Tree, Random Forest, and XGBoost were compared using five-fold stratified cross-validation on an 80\% development set, with PR-AUC used as the primary model-selection metric. XGBoost achieved the highest mean cross-validated PR-AUC of 0.4359 and was selected for subsequent evaluation. To adapt the classifier to a screening setting, the decision threshold was selected exclusively from out-of-fold development predictions under a prespecified Recall $\geq 0.80$ constraint, resulting in a threshold of 0.13 before the independent internal holdout set was examined. On the holdout set, the final model achieved a PR-AUC of 0.4238 and ROC-AUC of 0.8272; at the selected threshold, Recall reached 80.99\%, Precision 29.91\%, and F1-score 0.4369. The predicted probabilities had a Brier score of 0.0974, a calibration slope of 0.9590, and an intercept of $-0.0514$. SHAP ranked self-rated general health, high blood pressure, age, BMI, and high cholesterol as the five largest contributors to model predictions. Comparison with univariate statistical effect-size rankings showed seven shared features in the Top-10, with a Jaccard similarity of 0.5385 and a Spearman correlation of 0.7098, indicating partial rather than complete agreement between marginal association and multivariable predictive contribution. Taken together, the analysis separates model selection, threshold choice, holdout evaluation, calibration, and interpretation within a single development--evaluation sequence while avoiding the use of holdout performance to guide development decisions. The findings apply to the analyzed BRFSS sample and should not be interpreted as clinical diagnosis, causal evidence, or population-level estimates.
}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Diabetes is associated with cardiovascular, renal, neurological, and
vision-related complications, making early identification of individuals
at elevated risk relevant to screening and preventive care
\citep{ullah2022detecting,olisah2022diabetes,zhang2024prediabetes}.
The Behavioral Risk Factor Surveillance System (BRFSS) provides
self-reported demographic, behavioral, and health indicators that can be
used to study diabetes risk without laboratory measurements.
Machine-learning methods have therefore been applied to BRFSS and related
datasets using variables such as age, body mass index, blood pressure,
cholesterol status, physical activity, and self-rated health
\citep{xie2019diabetes,li2024gaxgboost,muhammad2025brfss}.

Model performance in this setting cannot be judged from Accuracy alone.
In the BRFSS 2015 dataset analyzed here, the positive class accounts for
only \textbf{13.93\%} of the records.
A classifier can therefore achieve high Accuracy while detecting only a
small proportion of positive cases.
Metrics such as PR-AUC and Recall are more informative when positive-case
detection is a central objective
\citep{saito2015precisionrecall}.
The classification threshold adds another decision point because increasing
Recall generally changes Precision, Specificity, and the number of
false-positive predictions.
For this reason, threshold selection should be separated from final
holdout evaluation rather than adjusted after observing test performance.

Discrimination alone also does not determine whether predicted
probabilities are reliable.
A model may rank positive cases above negative cases while still producing
probabilities that do not correspond closely to observed outcome
frequencies.
Calibration therefore needs to be evaluated separately using measures such
as the Brier score, calibration slope, and calibration intercept
\citep{huang2020calibration}.

Interpretability introduces a related but distinct issue.
SHAP can quantify how predictors contribute to the output of a fitted
model
\citep{lundberg2017unified,lundberg2020trees}, but a large SHAP value does
not imply a strong marginal statistical association or a causal effect.
Predictors may share information, interact with other variables, or become
more or less useful after other features are considered.
Comparing model-based importance with statistical effect sizes can therefore
show where these two forms of evidence agree and where they differ.

Based on these considerations, this study evaluates a single analysis
pipeline on the original imbalanced CDC BRFSS 2015 Diabetes Health
Indicators dataset.
Four classifiers are compared using five-fold stratified cross-validation
on a development set, with PR-AUC used for model selection.
The selected model is then assigned a screening threshold using only
out-of-fold development predictions under a prespecified Recall constraint.
After the model and threshold are fixed, performance is evaluated on an
independent internal holdout set using discrimination, classification,
bootstrap uncertainty, and calibration measures.
The final model is also examined with SHAP, and its global feature ranking
is compared with univariate statistical effect-size rankings.

The purpose of this workflow is not to introduce a new classifier or to
treat SHAP as clinical evidence.
Instead, the study examines whether model selection, threshold choice,
probability calibration, uncertainty estimation, and statistical
interpretation can be kept within one evaluation sequence without using the
holdout set to guide development decisions.
The reported findings are limited to the analyzed, unweighted BRFSS sample
and should not be interpreted as clinical diagnosis, causal evidence, or
population-level estimates.
All data-processing scripts, SQL validation queries, statistical analyses,
the machine-learning pipeline, calibration and SHAP analyses, figures, and
reproducibility checks are publicly available at
\url{https://github.com/AI-Peak/Diabetes-Analytics}.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

Machine learning has been widely applied to diabetes risk prediction using
survey, clinical, and laboratory data.
Using BRFSS 2014, Xie et al.~\citep{xie2019diabetes} compared several
classifiers, including Logistic Regression, Decision Tree, Random Forest,
Neural Network, and Support Vector Machine, and also examined adjusted
associations using multivariable regression.
More recent BRFSS-based studies have evaluated ensemble methods and
explainability techniques.
Li et al.~\citep{li2024gaxgboost} combined class-balancing methods,
GA-optimized XGBoost, stacking, and SHAP, while other studies have used
tree-based models and SHAP to examine diabetes-related predictors in BRFSS
data~\citep{majcherek2025aidriven,muhammad2025brfss}.
These studies show that both predictive modeling and model explanation are
already established components of BRFSS-based diabetes research; broader reviews
also emphasize substantial heterogeneity in datasets, preprocessing, validation,
and reporting practices~\citep{mohsen2023scoping}.

Evaluation choices remain important because diabetes datasets are commonly
imbalanced and different metrics describe different aspects of model
performance.
Precision--Recall analysis is more informative than ROC analysis alone when
the positive class is relatively uncommon~\citep{saito2015precisionrecall}.
Prediction-model guidance also distinguishes model development decisions
from final evaluation and recommends assessing performance beyond a single
discrimination metric~\citep{efthimiou2024developing,collins2024evaluation}.
Reporting and risk-of-bias guidance for prediction models further stresses transparent
separation of model development, evaluation, and applicability claims
~\citep{collins2024tripodai,moons2025probastai}.
Calibration is a separate property from discrimination and should be
examined when predicted probabilities are interpreted as risk estimates
\citep{huang2020calibration}.

Interpretability introduces a second distinction.
SHAP can quantify how individual features contribute to predictions from
tree-based models~\citep{lundberg2017unified,lundberg2020trees}, and related
healthcare studies have used SHAP to interpret diabetes and glycemic prediction
models~\citep{lugner2024predictors,prendin2023shap}. However, SHAP
importance is not equivalent to statistical association or causal effect.
A feature may have a strong marginal association with the outcome while
adding limited information after other predictors are considered, and a
feature with modest marginal association may still contribute to a
multivariable model.

Accordingly, the present study does not treat the use of XGBoost or SHAP as
a methodological novelty.
Its focus is the evaluation sequence applied to the original imbalanced
BRFSS 2015 sample: model selection by cross-validation, threshold selection
from out-of-fold development predictions, one-time internal holdout
evaluation, bootstrap uncertainty estimation, calibration assessment, and
comparison between SHAP importance and statistical effect-size rankings.
The statistical--SHAP comparison is used as an exploratory analysis of
agreement between marginal association and predictive contribution, not as
a validation of causal or clinical importance.

\subsection{Dataset and Data Validation}
\label{subsec:dataset}

The CDC BRFSS 2015 Diabetes Health Indicators dataset contained
\textbf{253,680 records} and 21 predictors~\citep{cdc2015brfss,uci2023cdc,teboul2021kaggle}.
The original data sources are documented in the project repository.
The binary target combined reported prediabetes and diabetes into Class 1,
while Class 0 represented no reported diabetes.
Class 1 contained \textbf{35,346 records (13.93\%)}, and Class 0 contained
218,334 records (86.07\%).

Validation checks covered missing values, data types, target values, and
feature ranges.
No missing or out-of-range values were identified.
Variables stored as floating-point values despite having integer-valued
entries were converted to integer format.

A total of \textbf{24,206 repeated feature profiles} were identified.
They were retained because the dataset contained no respondent identifier,
so identical feature profiles could not be confirmed as duplicate
individuals.

No SMOTE, oversampling, or undersampling was applied.
All analyses therefore used the observed class distribution.


\subsection{Statistical Association Analysis}
\label{subsec:stats}

Univariate tests were selected according to variable type.
Eighteen binary, categorical, or ordinal predictors were evaluated using
the Chi-Square Test of Independence.
Association strength was measured with Cram{\'e}r's $V$:

\begin{equation}
V =
\sqrt{
\frac{\chi^2}
{N \cdot \min(r-1,c-1)}
}.
\end{equation}

For interpretation, $V<0.05$ was treated as negligible,
0.05--$<0.10$ as weak,
0.10--$<0.20$ as small,
0.20--$<0.30$ as moderate,
and $\geq0.30$ as strong.

BMI, mental unhealthy days, and physical unhealthy days were treated as
numerical variables.
Group differences were evaluated using Welch's two-sample $t$-test and the
Mann--Whitney U test.
Cohen's $d$, rank-biserial correlation, and common-language effect size
were used to describe effect magnitude.
Absolute rank-biserial correlation was used when ranking numerical
predictors by univariate effect size.

Holm correction was applied to the 18 Chi-Square tests and the three
Mann--Whitney U tests.
Effect sizes were interpreted alongside adjusted $p$-values because the
large sample size could produce small $p$-values for weak associations.

A multivariable Logistic Regression model containing all 21 predictors was
also fitted to estimate adjusted associations.
Reported quantities included regression coefficients, adjusted odds
ratios, 95\% confidence intervals, Holm-adjusted $p$-values, and variance
inflation factors.
For binary predictors, an odds ratio compared values 1 and 0.
For ordinal or numerical predictors, it represented a one-unit increase in
the encoded variable.
These estimates were treated as sample-level associations and not as
causal effects.


\subsection{Model Development and Selection}
\label{subsec:ml}

The dataset was divided using a stratified 80/20 split with random state
42.
The development set contained \textbf{202,944 records}, and the independent
internal holdout set contained \textbf{50,736 records}.
The holdout set was excluded from model selection, feature selection,
threshold selection, and model-fitting decisions.

Four classifiers were compared using scikit-learn and XGBoost implementations~\citep{pedregosa2011scikit,chen2016xgboost}:
Logistic Regression, Decision Tree, Random Forest, and XGBoost.
Five-fold stratified cross-validation was performed only on the development
set.

For predictive Logistic Regression, BMI, mental unhealthy days, and
physical unhealthy days were standardized.
GenHlth, Age, Education, and Income were one-hot encoded, while binary
variables were retained without scaling.
Tree-based models used the original encoded feature values.

The prespecified model configurations were:
Logistic Regression with a maximum of 1,000 iterations;
Decision Tree with maximum depth 8;
Random Forest with 100 trees and maximum depth 12; and
XGBoost with 100 boosting rounds, maximum depth 6, and learning rate 0.1.

Accuracy, Precision, Recall, F1-score, ROC-AUC, and PR-AUC were calculated
for each fold.
\textbf{Mean PR-AUC was the primary model-selection metric} because Class 1
represented 13.93\% of the dataset.
Mean ROC-AUC and lower PR-AUC variability were prespecified as tie-breakers.


\subsection{Threshold Selection and Holdout Evaluation}
\label{subsec:threshold}

After model selection, out-of-fold probabilities were generated for every
record in the development set.
Candidate thresholds from 0.01 to 0.99 were evaluated without using the
holdout set.

Thresholds satisfying
\[
\mathrm{Recall} \geq 0.80
\]
were retained.
Among these candidates, the threshold with the highest Precision was
selected.
F1-score and then the higher threshold were used as tie-breakers.

After the model and threshold were fixed, the selected classifier was
trained on the complete development set and evaluated once on the
independent holdout set.
Reported metrics included Accuracy, Precision, Recall, Specificity,
F1-score, ROC-AUC, PR-AUC, and the confusion matrix.

Uncertainty was estimated with
\textbf{1,000 stratified bootstrap samples} drawn from the holdout set.
Percentile-based 95\% confidence intervals were calculated for ROC-AUC,
PR-AUC, Recall, Precision, and F1-score.


\subsection{Calibration and SHAP Analysis}
\label{subsec:calibration_shap}

Calibration was assessed on the holdout set using the Brier score,
calibration slope, calibration intercept, and a reliability curve based on
10 probability groups.
The slope and intercept were estimated from a logistic model relating the
observed outcome to the log-odds of the predicted probabilities.
No Platt scaling, isotonic regression, or other recalibration procedure was
applied.

SHAP was used to explain the final fitted model.
The original stratified split was reconstructed, and up to 10,000 holdout
records were sampled using random state 42.
For the selected tree-based model, TreeExplainer was used to calculate SHAP
values.
Global importance was defined as the mean absolute SHAP value for each
predictor.

The SHAP ranking was compared with the univariate effect-size ranking.
Cram{\'e}r's $V$ was used for categorical and ordinal predictors, while
absolute rank-biserial correlation was used for numerical predictors.
A marginal association was treated as non-negligible when
Cram{\'e}r's $V\geq0.05$ or
$|r_{\mathrm{rb}}|\geq0.10$.

Agreement was evaluated using Top-5, Top-10, and Top-15 overlap,
Jaccard similarity, and Spearman rank correlation.
This comparison was used to contrast marginal statistical association with
predictive contribution to the fitted model; it was not treated as causal
or clinical validation of SHAP.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

Three findings require interpretation.
First, XGBoost was selected under the prespecified cross-validation rule,
although its mean PR-AUC differed from Random Forest by only 0.0058.
Second, the development-selected threshold of 0.13 substantially changed
the balance between missed positive cases and false-positive predictions.
Third, SHAP and univariate effect-size rankings agreed on several major
predictors but did not produce identical rankings.


\subsection{Model Selection, Screening Threshold, and Calibration}
\label{subsec:model_discussion}

XGBoost had the highest mean cross-validated PR-AUC, but the absolute
difference from Random Forest was small.
The selection should therefore be interpreted as the outcome of the
prespecified model-selection rule rather than evidence of a large
performance advantage over Random Forest.

The threshold analysis produced a larger practical change than the choice
between the two highest-ranked models.
At the default threshold of 0.50, the model detected only 16.54\% of the
positive holdout records.
Using the development-selected threshold of
\textbf{0.13 increased Recall to 80.99\%}, while
\textbf{Precision decreased to 29.91\%}.
False negatives decreased from 5,900 to 1,344, whereas false positives
increased from 925 to 13,416.

This threshold therefore represents a screening-oriented operating point
under the study's prespecified Recall constraint.
It should not be interpreted as a general clinical cutoff.
A setting in which false-positive follow-up is costly may require a
different threshold.

The threshold was selected exclusively from out-of-fold development
predictions before holdout evaluation.
This separation prevents direct optimization of the decision threshold on
the reported holdout results.

Calibration estimates on the internal holdout were
a Brier score of 0.0974, a slope of \textbf{0.9590}, and an intercept of
$-0.0514$.
The slope and intercept were close to their reference values of 1 and 0,
respectively, within this holdout sample.
These estimates do not establish calibration in another year, population,
or clinical setting.
No recalibration procedure was evaluated in the present analysis.


\subsection{Statistical Associations and SHAP Explanations}
\label{subsec:shap_discussion}

The statistical and SHAP analyses identified several of the same
predictors near the top of their rankings.
\textit{GenHlth}, \textit{HighBP}, Age, BMI, and \textit{HighChol}
were among the highest-ranked predictors in the final XGBoost model and
also showed non-negligible univariate associations with the target.

However, the two analyses measure different quantities.
Univariate effect sizes describe marginal differences between target
classes, whereas SHAP values describe contributions to predictions made by
the fitted multivariable model.
Accordingly, exact agreement between the rankings is not expected.

The Top-10 rankings shared
\textbf{7 features}, with a
\textbf{Jaccard similarity of 0.5385}.
Across all 21 predictors, the Spearman rank correlation was
\textbf{$r_s=0.7098$}.

Several predictors illustrate the difference between the two ranking
systems.
\textit{DiffWalk} had a Cram{\'e}r's $V$ of 0.2183 but ranked 12th by SHAP,
and \textit{PhysHlth} had a rank-biserial correlation of 0.2260 but ranked
16th by SHAP.
Conversely, Sex ranked seventh by SHAP despite a marginal
Cram{\'e}r's $V$ of 0.0314.

These differences can arise when predictors contain overlapping
information or participate in nonlinear model structures, but the present
analysis does not identify the specific mechanism responsible for each
ranking difference.
SHAP values should therefore be interpreted as explanations of model
behavior rather than estimates of biological importance or causal effects.

The proposed framework emphasizes screening sensitivity rather than maximizing overall accuracy. On the same independent holdout set, selecting the threshold from development-set out-of-fold predictions increased Recall from 16.54\% to \textbf{80.99\%}, reducing missed positive cases by \textbf{77.22\%}. This improvement came at the cost of lower Precision (29.91\%) and more false-positive predictions. Although several previous BRFSS studies reported higher classification performance under different experimental conditions, the main contribution of this work lies in its leakage-controlled threshold selection, explicit screening trade-off, and reliability-oriented evaluation rather than superiority in cross-study predictive accuracy.

The main contribution of the present study is consequently the evaluation
sequence rather than a new classifier: cross-validated model selection on the
development set, threshold selection from out-of-fold development predictions,
one-time internal holdout evaluation, bootstrap uncertainty, calibration
assessment, and an explicitly exploratory comparison between SHAP rankings and
univariate effect sizes. This framing is consistent with current guidance that
emphasizes transparent model evaluation and reporting rather than performance
claims based on a single metric~\citep{collins2024tripodai,moons2025probastai}.

\subsection{Limitations and Future Work}
\label{subsec:limitations}

Several limitations affect the interpretation of the results.
BRFSS is cross-sectional and self-reported, so the observed associations
cannot establish disease causation or future diabetes onset.
The positive class combines reported prediabetes and diabetes, while
Class 0 indicates no reported diabetes rather than laboratory-confirmed
absence of disease.

The analyzed dataset also lacks survey weights and design variables,
respondent identifiers, and clinical biomarkers such as HbA1c or fasting
glucose.
The reported estimates therefore describe the analyzed unweighted sample
and should not be interpreted as population-level estimates for the United
States.

Evaluation was limited to an internal holdout from the same 2015 dataset.
The four predictive models used fixed configurations rather than nested
hyperparameter optimization.
In addition, repeated feature profiles were retained because respondent
identifiers were unavailable.
Identical profiles could therefore occur in both development and holdout
sets even when they represent different respondents.

The threshold of 0.13 was selected for the specific constraint
Recall $\geq 0.80$.
Its suitability depends on the relative cost of missed positive cases and
false-positive follow-up.
The SHAP alignment analysis also depends on the selected effect-size
definitions and Top-$k$ cutoffs.

Future evaluation should prioritize temporal or external validation,
including newer BRFSS waves or datasets with clinical biomarkers.
Additional analyses should examine subgroup performance, SHAP stability,
the effect of repeated profiles on validation estimates, survey-weighted
inference, and nested model tuning.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

A reliability-oriented and interpretable diabetes analytics workflow was developed using the original imbalanced CDC BRFSS 2015 dataset with 253,680 records. Repeated response profiles were retained because respondent identifiers were unavailable and identical records could not be confirmed as duplicate individuals. Among four evaluated models, XGBoost achieved the highest mean five-fold cross-validated PR-AUC of 0.4359 and was selected for final evaluation.

On the independent internal holdout set, XGBoost achieved a PR-AUC of 0.4238 and a ROC-AUC of 0.8272, with bootstrap 95\% confidence intervals of 0.4135--0.4347 and 0.8223--0.8322, respectively. The screening threshold of 0.13 was selected from out-of-fold development predictions before holdout evaluation. At this threshold, Recall reached 80.99\% (95\% CI: 80.04\%--81.94\%), Precision reached 29.91\% (95\% CI: 29.53\%--30.28\%), Specificity was 69.28\%, and F1-score reached 0.4369 (95\% CI: 0.4317--0.4418). False negatives decreased from 5,900 to 1,344, although lower Precision and Specificity produced more false-positive referrals. Calibration was reasonably stable, with a Brier score of 0.0974, a calibration slope of 0.9590, and an intercept of $-0.0514$.

Adjusted Logistic Regression results supported several sample-level associations after accounting for all predictors, while variance inflation factors remained below 1.80, indicating no severe multicollinearity. SHAP identified self-rated general health, high blood pressure, age, BMI, and high cholesterol as the most influential predictive features. Seven of the Top-10 SHAP and statistical effect-size features overlapped, producing a Jaccard similarity of 0.5385 and a Spearman correlation of 0.7098. This agreement was meaningful but incomplete, reflecting differences between marginal statistical association and multivariable predictive contribution. Overall, the workflow combines statistical analysis, leakage-controlled model and threshold selection, independent holdout evaluation, uncertainty estimation, calibration, and model explanation. The results support screening-oriented analysis but should not be interpreted as clinical diagnosis, causal evidence, or national population estimates. External validation on newer BRFSS waves, other populations, and datasets containing clinical biomarkers remains necessary.
